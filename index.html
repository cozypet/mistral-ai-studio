Your AI Demo Works. Now Prove It Won't Embarrass You in Production

A field guide to Mistral AI Studio: the loop that turns production traffic into a model that gets better instead of quietly worse.
Han HELOIR YAN, Ph.D. ☕️

A prompt tweak ships on a Tuesday. It looks fine in the playground. Three weeks later your support assistant has been telling customers to "check the manual" in a tone somewhere between bored and hostile, and nobody noticed because nobody was watching.

The first real signal is an apology email your head of support has to write to a key account. The model was capable the whole time. What you lacked was a way to see, score, and fix its behavior in production.

That gap is the entire reason Mistral AI Studio exists. Not "another place to call a model." A system for running one.

The one idea: a flywheel, not a dashboard

Most "observability" products stop at a dashboard. You get charts, you nod, nothing changes. AI Studio is built around a different shape: a loop where production usage becomes the fuel for the next improvement.

Here is the loop. You observe real traffic. You define what good looks like with an evaluator. You run that evaluator across thousands of past conversations. You collect the labeled results into a curated dataset. You use that dataset to measure and improve. Then the improved system ships, generates new traffic, and the loop turns again.

Mistral organizes this into three pillars. Observability is how you see and score behavior. Agent Runtime is how you execute work durably and reproducibly. AI Registry is the system of record that versions every asset and controls what reaches production. The pillars are not three separate tools bolted together. They are three faces of one loop.

Keep the loop in your head as you read. Every feature below earns its place by the part of the loop it powers. One honest caveat up front: the full Observability suite is currently an Enterprise tier capability, so some of what follows assumes that access.

Seeing production clearly

Back to the silent drift. The on call engineer cannot fix what she cannot find, and right now her only evidence is one angry email. Observability is the pillar that turns "something feels off" into a list of exact conversations she can read, score, and act on.

But none of it works until the data is flowing. So we start there.

How the data gets in (instrumentation)

The story. Your assistant has been live for months. You open Explorer hoping to read those bad conversations and the screen is empty, because nothing was ever instrumented. This is the most common day one mistake: people expect the platform to magically know about traffic it was never told about.

Why and when. You instrument once, at the start, so every call your application makes leaves a trace. The Mistral SDK ships an OpenTelemetry native tracing layer, which matters for two reasons: spans follow the GenAI semantic conventions other tools understand, and you are not locked into a proprietary wire format.

from mistralai import Mistral
from mistralai.extra.observability import configure_telemetry

client = Mistral(api_key="...")

# "dedicated": SDK owns an exporter that ships spans to Mistral.
# "global": reuse your existing OpenTelemetry provider.
# Or pass your own TracerProvider to fan out to Mistral and your stack.
configure_telemetry(client, provider="dedicated")

resp = client.chat.complete(
    model="mistral-large-latest",
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": "How do I reset my password?"}],
    # custom metadata becomes filterable fields in Explorer later
    # (attach use_case, tenant, route, version, and so on)
)

The metadata you attach here is not decoration. It becomes the fields you filter on when you are hunting a regression at 2am. Tag traffic with the use case, the tenant, the route, the prompt version. Future you will be grateful.

Explorer: read what actually shipped

The story. With traffic flowing, the engineer opens Explorer and filters to the support route, the last three weeks, only assistant turns longer than a sentence. There they are: forty conversations where the tone went cold right after the prompt change. She has gone from one apology email to the full population of the problem in two minutes.

Why and when. Reach for Explorer whenever you need to understand what is really happening in production, investigate a specific quality issue, or pull representative examples for later analysis. Filters compose as a tree, so "support route AND last 3 weeks AND (rude OR off topic)" is a structure, not a guess.

from mistralai import models

events = client.beta.observability.chat_completion_events.search(
    search_params=models.FilterPayload(
        filters=models.FilterGroup(
            and_=[
                models.FilterCondition(field="extra_fields.use_case",
                                       op="eq", value="customer_support"),
                models.FilterCondition(field="created_at",
                                       op="gte", value="2026-05-15T00:00:00Z"),
            ]
        )
    ),
    page_size=50,
)

The operator vocabulary is broad: eq, neq, gt, gte, lt, lte, contains, icontains, notcontains, startswith, endswith, matches, isnull, includes, excludes, len_eq. Compose them with nested and_ and or_ groups to slice traffic precisely.

There is a sharper move. Once she finds one bad conversation, she does not want to hand write a filter that catches every sibling. She asks Explorer for semantically similar events and the platform returns the cluster.

# Found one bad answer. Now pull everything that looks like it.
similar = client.beta.observability.chat_completion_events.fetch_similar_events(
    event_id="evt_abc123",
)

Judges: turn "this feels rude" into a number

The story. Forty conversations is readable. Forty thousand is not. The team needs to score tone automatically, the same way every time, so the judgment does not depend on who is reading or how tired they are. They write the rubric once as a Judge.

Why and when. Use a Judge when you have a quality question you will ask repeatedly: tone, factuality against a known answer, policy compliance, helpfulness. A Judge is an evaluator made of three parts: a model, instructions, and an output schema. Two output shapes exist. Classification returns a named label. Regression returns a number on a scale.

from mistralai import models

# Classification: assign a discrete label
tone_judge = client.beta.observability.judges.create(
    name="Support Tone",
    description="Is the assistant's tone acceptable for support?",
    model_name="mistral-large-latest",
    instructions=(
        "Read the assistant's reply. Classify its tone. "
        "PROFESSIONAL: warm and appropriate. "
        "CURT: technically correct but cold. "
        "RUDE: dismissive or hostile."
    ),
    tools=[],
    output=models.JudgeClassificationOutput(
        type="CLASSIFICATION",
        options=[
            models.JudgeClassificationOutputOption(value="PROFESSIONAL", description="Warm, appropriate"),
            models.JudgeClassificationOutputOption(value="CURT", description="Cold but correct"),
            models.JudgeClassificationOutputOption(value="RUDE", description="Dismissive or hostile"),
        ],
    ),
)

The trap with evaluators is spending real money running a flawed rubric across your whole history. So before scaling, test the Judge on a single record. The platform even gives Judges their own Playground for exactly this.

# Validate the rubric on ONE event before you spend anything
result = client.beta.observability.chat_completion_events.judge(
    event_id="evt_abc123",
    judge_id=tone_judge.id,
)

Campaigns: grade three months of traffic overnight

The story. The rubric works on one record. Now the team points it at a quarter of support traffic and goes home. By morning, every conversation in that window carries a tone label, and the size of the problem is no longer a feeling, it is a percentage.

Why and when. Reach for a Campaign when you need a Judge applied in bulk to a filtered slice of history: auditing behavior, tagging traffic by category, or building a quality labeled dataset. A Campaign uses exactly one Judge. It runs asynchronously, capped between 100 and 10,000 events, so you launch it and walk away.

campaign = client.beta.observability.campaigns.create(
    name="Q2 Support Tone Audit",
    description="Tone classification across Q2 support traffic",
    judge_id=tone_judge.id,
    max_nb_events=5000,
    search_params=models.FilterPayload(
        filters=models.FilterGroup(and_=[
            models.FilterCondition(field="extra_fields.use_case", op="eq", value="customer_support"),
            models.FilterCondition(field="created_at", op="gte", value="2026-04-01T00:00:00Z"),
            models.FilterCondition(field="created_at", op="lt",  value="2026-07-01T00:00:00Z"),
        ])
    ),
)

import time
while True:
    status = client.beta.observability.campaigns.fetch_status(campaign_id=campaign.id)
    if status.status in ("COMPLETED", "FAILED"):
        break
    time.sleep(10)

annotated = client.beta.observability.campaigns.list_events(campaign_id=campaign.id)

This is the step that kills the manual labeling bottleneck. Weeks of human annotation collapse into an overnight job.

Datasets: production traffic becomes a regression baseline

The story. The team now has 5,000 labeled conversations. The RUDE and CURT ones are gold: they are exactly the cases the next prompt must handle. They pull those into a named Dataset, fix a few messages by hand, write the ideal reply for each, and freeze it. From now on, every candidate change is tested against this set before it ships. The silent drift can never happen invisibly again, because this Dataset is the tripwire.

Why and when. Use a Dataset when you want a curated, editable, versioned collection of conversations to evaluate against or to keep as a regression baseline. Unlike raw Explorer traffic, Dataset records are editable. Each record carries structured properties: expected_output (the ideal reply a Judge can compare against), category, and grading_guidance.

dataset = client.beta.observability.datasets.create(
    name="support_tone_regression_2026_q2",
    description="Frozen baseline of hard tone cases",
)

# The flywheel made literal: import straight from the campaign you just ran
client.beta.observability.datasets.import_from_campaign(
    dataset_id=dataset.id, campaign_id=campaign.id,
)

# Or pull a filtered slice directly from Explorer
client.beta.observability.datasets.import_from_explorer(
    dataset_id=dataset.id,
    search_params=models.FilterPayload(
        filters=models.FilterCondition(field="extra_fields.use_case", op="eq", value="customer_support"),
    ),
)

# Export when you want the raw JSONL for an offline eval harness
client.beta.observability.datasets.export_to_jsonl(dataset_id=dataset.id)

Notice the import methods: import_from_explorer, import_from_campaign, import_from_playground, import_from_file. The flywheel is not a metaphor here. It is a method signature.

Closing the measurement loop

Above the four building blocks, AI Studio adds Experiments, Iterations, and Dashboards so improvement is measurable rather than anecdotal, plus lineage that traces an outcome back to the prompt, the prompt back to its version, and the version back to the real traffic that produced it. That lineage is what lets you say "accuracy went up 6 points because of change X," instead of "it feels better."

Agent Runtime

Observability tells you what happened. The Agent Runtime is where work actually happens, and it is the part most teams underestimate until it burns them.

The story that motivates everything here. Your support assistant grows up. It no longer just answers questions, it processes a refund: look up the order, check the policy, call the payments API, email the customer, log the case. You wired that as a Python script with five API calls in a row. It works in the demo. In production, the payments call times out on call number four. Now you have charged nothing but emailed the customer a confirmation, or refunded twice on a retry, and you have no record of which step failed or why. A sequence of API calls is not a system. It has no memory, no recovery, and no audit trail.

The Agent Runtime is the answer to that failure. It is the execution backbone that runs every agent, from a single answer to a long business process, with durability, transparency, and reproducibility. Under the hood it leans on a durable execution engine (Temporal), but you never have to think about that. What you get is the behavior.

Durability and reproducibility

Why it matters. When step four fails, a durable runtime does not lose the first three steps. It resumes from where it broke, retries with consistent behavior, and never double executes a step it already completed. The refund either fully happens or cleanly does not. That is the difference between a script and a system.

Stateful conversations

The story. A customer starts a return on Monday and replies to your follow up on Wednesday. With a stateless script you would stitch the history together by hand every time and pray you got the ordering right. The runtime keeps conversation state for you, so Wednesday's reply continues Monday's thread with full context.

Why and when. Use stateful conversations for anything that spans more than one turn or more than one sitting: support threads, multi step intake, anything with memory.

# Start a durable, stateful conversation
conv = client.beta.conversations.start(
    agent_id="agent_support_v3",
    inputs="I want to return order #4417.",
)

# Two days later, the customer replies. Append to the SAME conversation.
conv = client.beta.conversations.append(
    conversation_id=conv.conversation_id,
    inputs="Actually, can I exchange it instead?",
)

history = client.beta.conversations.get_history(conversation_id=conv.conversation_id)

Auditable execution graphs and large payloads

Why it matters. Every run produces a static graph of the path it took, which step called which tool, what came back. When the refund goes wrong, you do not guess. You open the graph and see exactly where it stopped. The runtime also offloads large documents to object storage instead of dragging them through every step, so a 200 page contract in the flow does not blow up your payloads.

The loop closes here

Why it matters. This is the quiet genius of the design. Every execution in the runtime emits telemetry and evaluation data straight back into Observability. The work your agents do becomes the traffic your Judges score becomes the Datasets that improve the next version. Runtime feeds Observability feeds improvement feeds Runtime. The flywheel turns without you carrying buckets between pillars.

Workflows

If the Agent Runtime is the engine, Workflows are what you actually build and operate on top of it. This is the working core of the runtime, so it deserves real attention, not a footnote.

The story. Your refund process is not one agent thinking. It is a defined business flow: validate the order, check eligibility, attempt the refund, notify the customer, escalate to a human if the amount is over a threshold. You want to define that flow once, run it thousands of times, schedule it, and inspect every run when finance asks "what happened to case 9981?" That is a Workflow.

Why and when. Reach for a Workflow when the process has structure that should outlive any single call: many steps, branching, retries, a schedule, or a need to inspect run history later. Reach for a plain agent call when the task is a single turn with no durability needs. The dividing line is whether you would be upset if the process forgot where it was halfway through.

Execute a workflow and wait for the result, or fire it and inspect later:

# Run a registered workflow and block until it finishes
result = client.workflows.execute_workflow_and_wait(
    name="refund_flow",
    inputs={"order_id": "4417", "amount": 89.90},
)

The part finance cares about is run history. Every execution is durable and inspectable.

# List recent runs of the workflow
runs = client.workflows.runs.list_runs()

# Pull the full step by step history of one run
history = client.workflows.runs.get_run_history(run_id="run_9981")
# -> each step, its inputs, outputs, retries, and final status

And because production flows rarely run on demand only, you schedule them. Nightly reconciliation, hourly syncs, a weekly digest.

# Schedule the workflow to run every night at 02:00
client.workflows.schedules.schedule_workflow(
    name="refund_reconciliation",
    # cron and payload config per the schedules API
)

# Pause and resume without deleting the schedule
client.workflows.schedules.pause_schedule(schedule_id="sched_123")
client.workflows.schedules.resume_schedule(schedule_id="sched_123")

Workflows are where durability stops being an abstract promise and becomes the thing that lets you sleep while a scheduled job retries a flaky downstream API at 3am and recovers on its own.

AI Registry

Definitions are boring, so here is what the Registry actually buys you, told as the moment you need it.

The story. You ship support agent v3 on Friday. By Saturday, your tone Dashboard is red: v3 is curt. In a world without a registry, "rolling back" means someone digging through commits, redeploying, and hoping. In AI Studio, production does not point at v3. It points at an alias named production that you can repoint to v2 in one call. The bad version is out of the line of fire in seconds, and every version is still on record for the postmortem.

Why it matters. The Registry is the system of record for every asset across the lifecycle: agents, models, datasets, judges, tools, and workflows. It tracks lineage, ownership, and versioning, and it enforces access controls, moderation policies, and promotion gates before anything reaches production. The concrete payoff lives in three SDK behaviors.

Versioning and aliases give you instant rollback. Agents are versioned, and you point a stable alias at whichever version is live.

# v3 looked great in the playground; promote it behind the "production" alias
client.beta.agents.create_version_alias(
    agent_id="agent_support", version=3, alias="production",
)

# Saturday: the tone dashboard is red. Repoint to v2 in one call.
client.beta.agents.update_version(
    agent_id="agent_support", version=2,
)
# Production now serves v2 again. v3 is still on record for the postmortem.

Access control decides who can touch shared assets. A shared knowledge library or a judge pack should not be editable by everyone. The Registry attaches access rules to assets.

# Grant a team read access to a shared library; keep write tight
client.beta.libraries.accesses.update(
    library_id="lib_policies",
    # share with an org/team at the desired level per the accesses API
)

Promotion gates and moderation policies stop a bad version reaching users. The Registry sits in front of deployment, so a version has to clear your gates (and your moderation policy) before it is promoted. That is the technical backbone of an AI governance story you can show an auditor: every output traces to a version, every version to the data that shaped it, every promotion to who approved it.

The rest of the toolbox, in context

AI Studio is more than the three pillars. Here is the rest, each with the one moment you reach for it.

Knowledge libraries and RAG. When the assistant must answer from your policy PDFs, not its training data, you upload documents to a library and let retrieval ground the answers. Libraries carry access control, so the policy set is governed like any other asset.

client.beta.libraries.documents.upload(
    library_id="lib_policies",
    file={"file_name": "returns_policy.pdf", "content": open("returns_policy.pdf","rb")},
)

Connectors. When the agent needs live data from an external system rather than a static document, connectors wire those sources in. Reach for them when "the answer depends on a system we already run." Path: client.beta.connectors.

Document AI (OCR). When the input arrives as a scanned invoice or a photographed form, OCR turns it into structured, layout aware text before anything else can reason over it. Path: client.ocr.

Audio, including realtime transcription. When the channel is voice, you transcribe (batch or streaming) and synthesize speech. Reach for it for call center flows and voice assistants. Paths: client.audio.transcriptions, client.audio.speech, plus realtime streaming in mistralai.extra.realtime.

Batch. When you have a million rows to process offline and latency does not matter, batch inference is far cheaper than hammering the live endpoint. Reach for it for backfills and bulk enrichment. Path: client.batch.

Codestral and FIM. When the use case is code, fill in the middle completes inside an existing file rather than only continuing from the end. Path: client.fim.

Embeddings. When you need semantic search or clustering under your own retrieval layer, you generate dense vectors. Path: client.embeddings.

Classifiers and Moderation. When you need a fast policy gate on inputs or outputs, the moderation classifiers flag unsafe content before it reaches a user. This is also a Registry citizen: moderation policy is enforced at promotion. Path: client.classifiers.

Vibe CLI. When you would rather scaffold agents and skills from your terminal than click through a console, the Vibe CLI is the code first entry point to the same platform.

Deployment and sovereignty

For a regulated buyer, this section is the one they read first, so do not bury it.

AI Studio supports hybrid, dedicated, and self hosted deployments, with full data ownership. You can run agents and workflows close to your own systems and migrate between environments without re architecting, while keeping the same durability, traceability, and control. For a European bank or a hospital, that combination of audit trail, governance, and the option to keep data on infrastructure they control is not a nice to have. It is the thing that decides whether the project is allowed to exist.

The honest part

No platform is free of trade offs, and pretending otherwise insults the reader.

The full Observability suite is Enterprise tier today, so smaller teams may not have access to the exact loop described here. A Judge is a model grading another model, which means your evaluator has its own error rate; always validate a Judge against a human labeled sample before you trust its numbers at scale. Campaigns cost real inference money, so a careless 10,000 event run on an expensive model is a bill, not a free audit. The durable runtime hides a lot of complexity, which is wonderful until you hit an edge case and have to reason about retries and idempotency anyway. Parts of the SDK live under the beta namespace and can move between versions, so pin your dependency. And while the platform leans toward lock in by design, the OpenTelemetry native tracing is a real escape hatch: your traces speak a standard other tools can read.

Run the loop once on something small

Do not try to operationalize your whole AI estate this quarter. Turn the flywheel exactly once, on one flow, and feel it work.

  1. Instrument one production flow with configure_telemetry and tag it with a use case.
  2. Open Explorer, filter to that flow, and read ten real conversations.
  3. Write one Judge for the single quality dimension you most worry about, and test it on one record.
  4. Run one small Campaign, capped at a few hundred events.
  5. Pull the worst results into one Dataset and freeze it as your regression baseline.
  6. Put your live agent behind a production alias so your next rollback is one call, not an afternoon.

That is the whole platform in miniature: see, score, curate, govern, repeat. Do it once and the gap between "the demo worked" and "it runs in production" stops being a leap of faith and becomes a loop you can turn on demand.
